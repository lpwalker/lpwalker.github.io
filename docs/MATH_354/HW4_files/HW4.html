Homework 4 – Regression Consult

When people make decisions, their choices are often swayed by how information is presented. This is often described as “the framing effect.” One example of this, is how commercials describe new medications. As an example, Nereus (tradipitant), a new drug for motion-induced vomiting like sea-sickness, is marketed with a positive frame as reducing vomiting incidence from 44% to 18% (a reduction of 60%). This marketing makes it seem more effective than a negative frame that says just 18% of patients still experienced vomiting, even though both statements are true.

Xu (2024) aimed to evaluate whether older adults are more or less vulnerable to framing than younger adults. They collected a sample of 696 adults aged 18 to 71 and exposed them to three different scenarios

Scenario 1: Rate Drug Efficacy (1 = very bad, 6 = very good)

  • Positive Frame: 70 out of 100 patients felt better
  • Negative Frame: 30 out of 100 patients did not feel better

Scenario 2: Likelihood to stop drinking cocoa (1 = surely stop, 6 = surely continue)

  • Positive Frame: Stopping drinking hot cocoa reduces cadmium and cancer risk.
  • Negative Frame: Continuing to drink hot cocoa increases cadmium and cancer risk.

Scenario 3: Preference for chance choice (1 = surely safe option, 6 = surely risky)

  • Positive Frame: Choice between 200 saved vs 1/3 chance all 600 are saved.
  • Negative Frame: Choice between 400 die vs 2/3 chance all 600 die.

In each scenario, participants were randomly assigned a positive or negative frame. They rated their response on a 1-6 scale. The researchers want to build three models, one for each scenario adjusting for simultaneous linear changes in demographic variables (gender, ethnicity, education level, political affiliation).

They want to evaluate whether, as adults age, the way choices are worded have more influence on their judgments and ultimate choices. If there is a change, are those changes driven by positive or negative framings? At what age does this effect onset?

Your Goals:

  • Determine the correct analysis strategy and conduct the necessary analyses.
    • Hint 1: Ensure to assess and communicate results about the model fit.
    • Hint 2: Ensure to think about whether or which interactions need to be included.
    • Hint 3: You will need run various post hoc procedures (e.g., emmeans, emtrends or johnson_neyman).
    • Hint 4: Looking at the paper may be helpful. They do include R code, but I expect you to do a much better job using the techniques we discussed (they do somethings differently)
  • Write a clear, concise, complete, and correct report conveying an answer to their research question.

The data are in HW4Data.csv but they will require some manual and tidyverse cleaning.

When I loaded the .csv file, there were extra columns and rows that were empty and needed to be removed. Additionally the first two rows were the full question followed by a shortened name for the column, so I made the shortened name the column header and removed the row containing the survey question, and removed columns that wouldn’t be useful to our analysis, including reCAPTCHA score, confirmation, language, distribution style, and start and end date. Next, like the paper, I removed participants who didn’t pass the attention and age attention checks, then removed the columns. Lastly, I chose to remove patients who answered “prefer not to say” for a question (n = 10) and fixed some of the wordings of the framing columns so it was easier for me to understand.

For each scenario {, , }$, we fit

\[ \begin{aligned} \widehat{Y}_{i,s} = \;& \beta_0 + \beta_1 I(\text{Frame}_{i} = \text{Pos}) + \beta_2 \,\text{Age}_i + \beta_3 \, I(\text{Frame}_{i} = \text{Pos}) \times \text{Age}_i \\ &+ \beta_4 I(\text{Gender}_i = \text{Male}) + \beta_5 I(\text{Gender}_i = \text{Non-binary}) \\ &+ \beta_6 I(\text{Eth}_i = \text{Asian}) + \beta_7 I(\text{Eth}_i = \text{Black}) + \beta_8 I(\text{Eth}_i = \text{Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander}) \\ &+ \beta_9 I(\text{Eth}_i = \text{Other}) + \beta_{10} I(\text{Eth}_i = \text{White}) \\ &+ \beta_{11} I(\text{Pol}_i = \text{Independent}) + \beta_{12} I(\text{Pol}_i = \text{Republican}) + \beta_{13} I(\text{Pol}_i = \text{Something else}) \\ &+ \beta_{14} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{High school}) + \beta_{15} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{Some college}) + \beta_{16} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{2-year}) \\ &+ \beta_{17} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{4-year}) + \beta_{18} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{Professional}) + \beta_{19} I(\text{Edu}_i = \text{Doctorate}) \end{aligned} \]

where \(\widehat{Y}_{i}\) is participant \(i\)’s predicted 1–6 rating in each scenario and \(I(\cdot)\) equals 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The reference participant is a Female, American Indian or Alaska Native, Democrat with Less than a high school education who saw the negative frame. Each model has \(p = 20\) coefficients.

# Residual plots: Q-Q plot and residuals vs fitted values (+/- 2 and 3 sigma)
resid_plots <- function(mod) {
  d <- augment(mod)
  s <- sigma(mod)
  qq <- ggplot(d, aes(sample = .resid)) +
    stat_qq(alpha = 0.4) + stat_qq_line() +
    labs(x = "Theoretical Quantiles", y = "Observed Quantiles") +
    theme_bw()
  rf <- ggplot(d, aes(.fitted, .resid)) +
    geom_point(alpha = 0.4) +
    geom_hline(yintercept = 0, linetype = "dashed", color = "deeppink3") +
    geom_hline(yintercept = c(-2, 2) * s, linetype = "dotted", color = "steelblue") +
    geom_hline(yintercept = c(-3, 3) * s, linetype = "dotted", color = "darkorange") +
    labs(x = expression("Fitted Values (" * hat(Y) * ")"), y = "Residual") +
    theme_bw()
  list(qq = qq, rf = rf)
}
 
# Influence plots: leverage, Cook's distance, DFFITS, studentized residuals
influence_plots <- function(mod) {
  n <- nobs(mod); p <- length(coef(mod))
  d <- tibble(obs = seq_len(n),
              Leverage                 = hatvalues(mod),
              `Cook's Distance`        = cooks.distance(mod),
              DFFITS                   = dffits(mod),
              `Studentized Residual`   = rstudent(mod)) |>
    pivot_longer(-obs, names_to = "measure") |>
    mutate(measure = fct_inorder(measure))
  cuts <- bind_rows(
    tibble(measure = "Leverage",             y = c(2, 3) * p / n,           level = c("Moderate", "High")),
    tibble(measure = "Cook's Distance",      y = qf(c(0.2, 0.5), p, n - p), level = c("Moderate", "High")),
    tibble(measure = "DFFITS",               y = c(-2, 2, -3, 3) * sqrt(p / n),
           level = rep(c("Moderate", "High"), each = 2)),
    tibble(measure = "Studentized Residual", y = c(-2, 2, -3, 3),
           level = rep(c("Moderate", "High"), each = 2))
  ) |> mutate(measure = factor(measure, levels = levels(d$measure)))
  ggplot(d, aes(obs, value)) +
    geom_point(size = 0.6, alpha = 0.6) +
    geom_hline(data = cuts, aes(yintercept = y, color = level), linetype = "dotted") +
    facet_wrap(~ measure, scales = "free_y") +
    scale_color_manual(values = c(Moderate = "steelblue", High = "darkorange")) +
    labs(x = "Observation Number", y = NULL, color = "Cutoff") +
    theme_bw()
}
 
# Coefficient table with standardized coefficients
coef_table <- function(mod) {
  tidy(mod) |>
    mutate(std = standardize_parameters(mod)$Std_Coefficient,
           p.value = if_else(p.value < 0.0001, "<0.0001", as.character(round(p.value, 4)))) |>
    kable(digits = 3, col.names = c("Term", "Estimate", "SE", "t", "p", "Std-Coef"))
}
 
# Partial omega-squared for each term in the (Type I) ANOVA
term_effects <- function(mod) {
  omega_squared(anova(mod)) |>
    as_tibble() |>
    mutate(Size = as.character(interpret_omega_squared(Omega2_partial, rules = "cohen1992"))) |>
    select(Parameter, Omega2_partial, CI_low, CI_high, Size) |>
    kable(digits = 3, col.names = c("Term", "Partial omega^2", "Lower", "Upper", "Size"))
}

# Key numbers for inline text
key_stats <- function(mod, frame) {
  s   <- summary(mod)
  int <- tidy(mod) |> filter(str_detect(term, ":age"))
  emt <- emtrends(mod, specs = frame, var = "age") |> test() |> as.data.frame()
  list(n = nobs(mod), r2 = s$r.squared, adj = s$adj.r.squared,
       F = s$fstatistic[1], df1 = s$fstatistic[2], df2 = s$fstatistic[3],
       Fp = pf(s$fstatistic[1], s$fstatistic[2], s$fstatistic[3], lower.tail = FALSE),
       int_b = int$estimate, int_p = int$p.value,
       neg_b = emt$age.trend[1], neg_p = emt$p.value[1],
       pos_b = emt$age.trend[2], pos_p = emt$p.value[2])
}

4 Discussion

4.1 Discussion

Across all three scenarios, the framing effect shows that the gap between the positive and negative frames widens with age. The widening comes almost entirely from the negative frame. In every scenario, the positive-frame age slope was essentially zero. Under the negative frame, older adults rated the drug as less effective, were more likely to say they would stop drinking cocoa, and preferred the risky program more. The strength of the evidence differs by scenario. The frame × age interaction was statistically discernible only for risky-choice framing. For attribute and goal framing, it was in the predicted direction but not discernible at \(\alpha = 0.05\). All three interactions had very small effect sizes. On the question of onset, the framing effect in the attribute scenario was present at every age from 18 to 71, so it has no onset age. For goal and risky-choice framing, the Johnson-Neyman analysis showed that the framing effect was not discernible among the youngest adults and became discernible around 26 or 27, persisting through to 71 (the oldest age).

These findings matter because older adults increasingly make high-stakes health and financial decisions, and much of the information they receive is framed in terms of losses. Our results suggest that loss-framed messages, rather than gain-framed ones, are the ones whose influence grows with age. Communicators such as clinicians, pharmaceutical advertisers, and advertising campaigns could therefore shift older adults’ judgments more by choosing negative wording, even when the information and outcome is the same.

Our conclusions are more cautious than Xu (2024), and the differences come from several changes to the analysis:

  • Data cleaning: The paper reported excluding participants who failed the attention check or gave mismatched ages, yet its N = 696 equals every completed survey in the data. We applied those exclusions, removing 8 participants, and treated “Prefer not to say” as missing rather than as a demographic group.
  • Model conditions: The paper reported no checks of model conditions. We assessed residuals, influence, and multicollinearity for each model and found non-Gaussian, unequal-variance errors caused by the bounded 1–6 scale, which we addressed with a Box-Cox transformation.
  • Frame-specific slopes: The paper estimated these by fitting separate regressions to each frame’s subset without the demographic controls. We estimated them with emtrends() from the full adjusted model.
  • Interpreting the interaction: The paper treated interactions with p = .087 and .111 as evidence that age moderates framing in every scenario. Our results support that claim only for risky-choice framing.

This study has several limitations. The participants were an MTurk convenience sample and were mostly White, college-educated, and Democrat, so the results may not generalize to all U.S. adults. The age distribution of the participants skewed younger, with less being closer to 71 and more being closer to the 20s. Few participants were over 60, and none were over 71.
